## Introduction

In the heart of West Africa, within the lush forests of what is now Ghana, there existed a powerful empire that would become one of the most formidable African states to resist European colonialism. This was the Ashanti Empire, and at the center of its identity, power, and spiritual existence sat an object of immense sacred importance—the Golden Stool, known in the Twi language as the *Sika Dwa Kofi*.

The story of the Golden Stool is not merely a tale of a golden throne. It is a profound narrative about identity, sovereignty, spiritual beliefs, and the clash between African kingdoms and European imperial ambitions. When the British Empire sought to possess this sacred object, they unknowingly ignited a conflict that would demonstrate the depth of the Ashanti people's devotion to their cultural heritage and their willingness to fight to protect it.

This book explores the rich history of the Golden Stool, from its mythical origins to its central role in one of the most significant anti-colonial uprisings in African history.

## The Birth of the Golden Stool

### The Legend of Okomfo Anokye

According to Ashanti tradition, the Golden Stool did not come into existence through human craftsmanship alone. Its origin is deeply rooted in spiritual mythology, making it far more than a mere piece of regalia.

In the late 17th century, around 1701, the Ashanti people were a collection of small states in the forest region of present-day Ghana. The visionary leader Osei Tutu, with the help of his chief priest and advisor Okomfo Anokye, sought to unite these states into a powerful confederation.

The legend tells that on a chosen Friday (hence the name *Kofi*, meaning "born on Friday"), Okomfo Anokye called upon the gods in a great ceremony. In the presence of a vast assembly of chiefs and people, he commanded a golden stool to descend from the sky and gently land upon the knees of Osei Tutu. This miraculous event was witnessed by thousands and was interpreted as divine sanction for Osei Tutu's leadership and the unification of the Ashanti states.

### The Spiritual Significance

Okomfo Anokye declared that the Golden Stool contained the *sunsum* (soul or spirit) of the entire Ashanti nation. Unlike other stools used by chiefs throughout the region—which represented the souls of individual rulers—the Golden Stool embodied the collective soul of all Ashanti people, past, present, and future.

The priest further proclaimed several sacred prohibitions:
- The Golden Stool must never touch the ground
- No one, not even the Asantehene (king), may sit directly upon it
- It must be protected at all costs, for its destruction would mean the destruction of the Ashanti nation itself

These prohibitions would later become central to the conflict with the British, who fundamentally misunderstood the nature of the stool's significance.

## The Significance of the Golden Stool

### More Than a Throne

To understand why the Ashanti people were willing to go to war over the Golden Stool, one must first understand what it represented to them. In European monarchy, a throne is a symbol of royal power and authority—an object upon which kings and queens sit. The Golden Stool, however, operated on an entirely different spiritual and philosophical plane.

The Golden Stool was not a seat of power but a vessel of the national soul. The Asantehene did not sit on the Golden Stool but sat beside it on his own stool. The Golden Stool would be placed on its own throne, sometimes seated on a specially crafted chair, covered with a blanket, and treated with the reverence accorded to a living entity.

### The Stool's Physical Description

The Golden Stool is approximately 46 centimeters (18 inches) high and 61 centimeters (24 inches) wide. It is made of solid gold over a wooden frame and is adorned with golden bells and fetishes. Over the centuries, additional gold ornaments and attachments were added to commemorate important events and victories.

The stool features the curved seat typical of Akan stools, supported by a central column and a curved base. Its weight and the purity of its gold have always been kept secret, adding to its mystique.

### The Stool in Ashanti Governance

The Golden Stool played a central role in Ashanti political life:

1. **Installation of Kings**: When a new Asantehene was to be installed, he would be ceremonially lowered and raised three times over the Golden Stool (without touching it), symbolizing his acceptance of the responsibilities of kingship and his connection to the national soul.

2. **Important Ceremonies**: The Golden Stool would appear at major state occasions, including the Odwira festival, a time of national purification and renewal.

3. **Justice and Law**: Oaths sworn on the Golden Stool were considered the most sacred and binding. To break such an oath was considered one of the gravest offenses.

4. **Unity**: The Golden Stool served as the ultimate symbol of Ashanti unity, binding together the various states of the confederation.

## The Rise of the Ashanti Empire

### From Confederation to Empire

With the Golden Stool as their unifying symbol, the Ashanti states under Osei Tutu rapidly transformed from a loose confederation into one of the most powerful empires in West African history. The first task was to throw off the domination of the Denkyira kingdom, to which the Ashanti had been tributaries.

In 1701, the newly unified Ashanti army defeated Denkyira at the Battle of Feyiase, capturing the Denkyira treasury and, importantly, the note of agreement the Denkyira had with the European trading companies at the coast. This gave the Ashanti direct access to the lucrative coastal trade.

### Expansion and Consolidation

Over the next century, the Ashanti Empire expanded dramatically:

- **Osei Tutu's reign (c. 1701-1717)**: Established the foundation of the empire and its institutions
- **Opoku Ware I (1720-1750)**: Expanded the empire to its greatest territorial extent, conquering Dagomba, Gonja, and other northern states
- **Osei Kwadwo (1764-1777)**: Reformed the administration, creating a more centralized bureaucratic state
- **Osei Bonsu (1801-1824)**: The empire reached its height of power and came into increasing conflict with British interests

### The Ashanti Military

The Ashanti developed a formidable military machine that would challenge European armies for over a century:

- **Organization**: The army was organized into wings (the right, left, advance guard, main body, and rear guard) that could operate independently or in coordination
- **Tactics**: The Ashanti perfected the art of forest warfare, using ambush, encirclement, and rapid movement
- **Weapons**: Initially using traditional weapons, the Ashanti quickly adopted and adapted European firearms, becoming skilled marksmen
- **Numbers**: At its peak, the Ashanti could field armies of 40,000 to 80,000 warriors

### Economy and Trade

The Ashanti Empire controlled rich gold-producing regions, which gave it considerable economic power:

- **Gold**: The empire's goldfields produced substantial quantities of the precious metal, which was used for trade and for adorning the royal court
- **Slaves**: Tragically, the slave trade was a significant part of the Ashanti economy, with war captives sold to European traders at the coast
- **Kola nuts**: These stimulants were traded north across the Sahara
- **Crafts**: Ashanti craftsmen produced renowned works in gold, wood, and textiles, including the famous kente cloth

## British Colonial Interests in the Gold Coast

### Early Contact

European contact with the Gold Coast (as Ghana was then known) began in the 15th century with Portuguese traders. By the 17th century, several European nations had established trading posts along the coast, including the British, Dutch, Danish, and others.

Initially, the primary interest was in gold, which gave the region its name. However, from the 17th century onward, the slave trade became the dominant commercial activity. The Ashanti Empire was a major supplier of slaves, captured in wars with neighboring peoples.

### The Shift from Trade to Empire

The 19th century brought significant changes:

1. **Abolition of the Slave Trade**: Britain abolished the slave trade in 1807 and began actively suppressing it, fundamentally changing the economic relationship with African kingdoms

2. **Legitimate Commerce**: There was a shift toward trade in palm oil, gold, and other commodities

3. **Strategic Competition**: European powers increasingly competed for territorial control rather than just trading rights

4. **The "Scramble for Africa"**: By the 1880s, European powers were actively dividing Africa among themselves, with little regard for existing political structures

### British Expansion on the Gold Coast

British presence gradually expanded from coastal trading posts:

- **1821**: The British government took over administration of the coastal forts from trading companies
- **1844**: The "Bond of 1844" brought coastal Fante states under British protection
- **1850**: The Gold Coast was formally constituted as a British colony
- **1874**: After the Third Anglo-Ashanti War, the southern portions of the Ashanti Empire came under British control

This expansion brought the British into inevitable conflict with the Ashanti Empire.

## The Anglo-Ashanti Wars

### First Anglo-Ashanti War (1824-1831)

The first major military conflict between the British and the Ashanti occurred when the British gave protection to groups fleeing Ashanti authority. In 1824, a British force under Sir Charles MacCarthy invaded Ashanti territory but was ambushed and destroyed at the Battle of Nsamankow. MacCarthy himself was killed, and according to some accounts, his skull was made into a drinking cup.

The Ashanti continued to press their advantage but were eventually defeated at the Battle of Dodowa in 1826. A treaty in 1831 established a buffer zone between British and Ashanti territories.

### Second Anglo-Ashanti War (1863-1864)

Tensions over control of the coastal region led to renewed conflict. However, the fighting was inconclusive, and no significant territorial changes resulted.

### Third Anglo-Ashanti War (1873-1874)

This war marked a turning point. In 1873, a British force under General Garnet Wolseley invaded the Ashanti homeland. In February 1874, British troops entered Kumasi, the Ashanti capital, for the first time.

The Asantehene Kofi Karikari had fled, and the British found the city largely deserted. They systematically looted the royal palace, taking numerous treasures back to Britain—but not the Golden Stool, which had been safely hidden.

The British then burned much of Kumasi before withdrawing. The resulting Treaty of Fomena forced the Ashanti to:
- Renounce claims to certain southern territories
- Pay an indemnity of 50,000 ounces of gold
- End human sacrifice
- Allow free trade and open roads

Though weakened, the Ashanti Empire survived with its core territory and, crucially, with the Golden Stool intact.

### Fourth Anglo-Ashanti War (1895-1896)

By the 1890s, European powers were scrambling to claim African territories. The British were concerned about French expansion from the west and German expansion from the east. They decided that the Ashanti Empire must become a British protectorate.

In 1895, a British force marched on Kumasi. The Asantehene Prempeh I, advised that resistance was futile, submitted without fighting. On January 20, 1896, in a humiliating ceremony, Prempeh I was forced to embrace the feet of the British governor—a profound insult to Ashanti dignity.

Prempeh I, the Queen Mother, and other important figures were arrested and exiled, first to Sierra Leone and later to the Seychelles Islands, where they would remain for over two decades.

Despite this conquest, the British made a crucial error: they assumed they had completely subjugated the Ashanti people. They were wrong.

## The Demand for the Golden Stool

### Governor Hodgson's Fateful Decision

In 1900, the new British Governor of the Gold Coast, Sir Frederick Hodgson, decided to visit Kumasi. He saw an opportunity to complete what he viewed as unfinished business: obtaining the Golden Stool.

On March 28, 1900, Hodgson assembled the chiefs and elders of the Ashanti nation at Kumasi. What he said next would ignite a war:

> "Where is the Golden Stool? Why am I not sitting on the Golden Stool at this moment? I am the representative of the paramount power; why have you relegated me to this chair? Why did you not take the opportunity of my coming to Kumasi to bring the Golden Stool, and give it to me to sit upon?"

### A Profound Misunderstanding

Hodgson's demand revealed a complete misunderstanding of the Golden Stool's nature and significance:

1. **Not a Throne**: The British thought the Golden Stool was simply the Ashanti king's throne. They did not understand that even the Asantehene never sat upon it.

2. **Symbol of Conquest**: The British saw possession of the stool as the logical conclusion of their conquest—taking the "throne" of a defeated king.

3. **Colonial Mentality**: The idea that an African religious object could be so sacred as to be non-negotiable was beyond colonial comprehension.

### The Ashanti Response

The immediate response to Hodgson's speech was diplomatic silence—a dangerous sign that the British failed to recognize. The chiefs stated that they needed time to discuss the matter among themselves.

In reality, Hodgson's demand had united the Ashanti people in a way that nothing else could have. The chiefs began to plan resistance, secretly organizing for war. The message spread throughout Ashanti territory: the British wanted to steal the soul of the nation.

---

## The War of the Golden Stool (1900)

### The Uprising Begins

The Ashanti response came swiftly. In early April 1900, Ashanti forces began attacking isolated British outposts and patrols throughout the territory. The telegraph lines to the coast were cut, and the roads were blocked.

Governor Hodgson and his wife, along with several hundred British soldiers, missionaries, and others, found themselves trapped in the small Kumasi fort. What followed was one of the most dramatic sieges in African colonial history.

### Ashanti War Objectives

The Ashanti had clear objectives:

1. **Protect the Golden Stool**: The stool was hidden in a secret location known only to a few trusted individuals
2. **Expel the British**: The Ashanti sought to drive the British out of their territory entirely
3. **Restore Independence**: They hoped to restore the Ashanti Empire to its former glory
4. **Free Prempeh I**: The return of the exiled Asantehene was a key goal

### British Strengths and Weaknesses

**Strengths:**
- Superior firepower (Maxim guns, artillery, modern rifles)
- Professional military training
- Access to reinforcements (eventually)
- Fortified positions

**Weaknesses:**
- Small numbers (initially only about 700 men in Kumasi)
- Hostile terrain (dense forest, unfamiliar ground)
- Disease (malaria and other tropical diseases would kill more soldiers than combat)
- Long supply lines
- Underestimation of Ashanti determination

### Ashanti Strengths and Weaknesses

**Strengths:**
- Massive numerical superiority (estimates range from 20,000 to 40,000 warriors)
- Fighting on home ground
- High motivation (fighting for their nation's soul)
- Experience in forest warfare
- Unity of purpose

**Weaknesses:**
- Limited modern weapons and ammunition
- No artillery
- Traditional tactics that were vulnerable to modern firepower
- Decentralized command structure

## The Siege of Kumasi

### The Fort at Kumasi

The Kumasi fort was a small defensive position, never designed to withstand a major siege. Within its walls, Governor Hodgson had:

- Approximately 700 soldiers (British regulars and African troops)
- Several hundred civilians, including women and children
- Limited supplies of food and ammunition
- A handful of small artillery pieces

### The First Weeks

The siege began in earnest in early April 1900. The Ashanti forces surrounded Kumasi and began systematic attacks:

- **Ambushes**: Any patrol venturing outside the fort was attacked
- **Sniping**: Ashanti marksmen kept up constant fire on the fort
- **Cutting supplies**: All roads and supply routes were blocked
- **Psychological warfare**: War drums and horns sounded through the night

Despite brave sorties by the British troops, they could not break the siege.

### Conditions Inside the Fort

As weeks turned into months, conditions inside the fort deteriorated:

- **Food**: Rations were cut repeatedly. Horses and mules were slaughtered for meat. Eventually, even rats became a food source.
- **Disease**: Malaria and dysentery swept through the besieged garrison. Medical supplies ran low.
- **Ammunition**: Despite rationing, the defenders' ammunition supply dwindled steadily.
- **Morale**: Though the British and their African allies fought bravely, the situation seemed increasingly hopeless.

### Failed Relief Attempts

The British made several attempts to relieve Kumasi:

- **Captain Hall's Column**: A small relief force was turned back with heavy losses
- **Other Attempts**: Several columns were defeated or forced to retreat

The dense forest, Ashanti ambushes, and the sheer numbers of the opposition made relief extremely difficult.

### The Escape

By June 1900, Governor Hodgson concluded that if they stayed, everyone would die—from starvation if not from Ashanti bullets. He made the difficult decision to attempt a breakout.

On June 23, 1900, the surviving garrison attempted to fight their way to the coast. The breakout force included:

- Governor Hodgson and his wife
- About 600 soldiers
- Approximately 400 civilians

They left behind a small rearguard of about 100 soldiers to defend the fort and the many sick and wounded who could not travel.

The escape was a nightmare journey. The column was attacked constantly, fighting battle after battle through the forest. Governor Hodgson's wife, Lady Mary Hodgson, endured the march with remarkable fortitude, despite illness and the constant danger.

After an agonizing journey, the survivors reached the coast. Behind them, the rearguard at Kumasi held out until a relief force finally arrived on July 15, 1900.

## Queen Mother Yaa Asantewaa

### An Extraordinary Leader

One of the most remarkable figures of the War of the Golden Stool was Yaa Asantewaa, the Queen Mother of Ejisu. Her leadership and courage would make her a legend in African history and a symbol of resistance to colonialism.

### Background

Yaa Asantewaa was born around 1840 in Besease, in the Ejisu district of the Ashanti region. She became Queen Mother of Ejisu when her brother Nana Akwasi Afrane Okpese became chief. In the Ashanti system, the Queen Mother was not merely a ceremonial figure but had real political power, including a role in selecting the chief.

When her brother died, Yaa Asantewaa's grandson was installed as the new chief. However, he was among those exiled by the British in 1896, along with Prempeh I.

### The Speech That Rallied a Nation

When Governor Hodgson demanded the Golden Stool, a council of chiefs met to discuss their response. According to tradition, some of the male chiefs wavered, uncertain whether to fight the powerful British.

It was then that Yaa Asantewaa stood and delivered a speech that would echo through history:

> "Now I see that some of you fear to go forward to fight for our king. If it was in the brave days of old, chiefs would not sit down to see their king to be taken away without firing a shot. No European could have dared speak to chiefs of Asante in the way the governor spoke to you this morning. Is it true that the bravery of Asante is no more? I cannot believe it. It cannot be! I must say this: if you, the men of Asante, will not go forward, then we will. We, the women, will. I shall call upon my fellow women. We will fight! We will fight till the last of us falls in the battlefields."

### Commander-in-Chief

Following her speech, the chiefs appointed Yaa Asantewaa as commander-in-chief of the Ashanti forces. It was an extraordinary appointment—a woman leading men in battle—but Yaa Asantewaa proved more than capable.

She organized the siege of Kumasi, coordinating the various Ashanti contingents and directing military operations. Her headquarters were at Ejisu, from where she oversaw the campaign.

### Capture and Exile

The war ultimately turned against the Ashanti. British reinforcements, including West African troops from other colonies and British regulars, poured into the region. By late 1900, the Ashanti resistance had been broken.

Yaa Asantewaa was captured in 1901 and, like Prempeh I before her, was exiled to the Seychelles Islands. She died there in 1921, never returning to her homeland.

### Legacy

Yaa Asantewaa's legacy endures:

- She is celebrated as a symbol of Ghanaian resistance to colonialism
- Her story is taught in Ghanaian schools
- The Yaa Asantewaa Museum in Ghana honors her memory
- She has been the subject of plays, songs, and other artistic works
- Her image has appeared on Ghanaian stamps and currency
- Her story inspires women's leadership movements across Africa

## The Aftermath and Legacy

### The Cost of War

The War of the Golden Stool was costly for both sides:

**British casualties:**
- Approximately 1,000 soldiers killed, mostly from disease
- Hundreds wounded
- Massive financial cost

**Ashanti casualties:**
- Estimates range from 2,000 to 3,000 killed
- Thousands more wounded
- Many villages destroyed
- Crops and livestock destroyed, leading to famine

### Political Consequences

**For the Ashanti:**
- The Ashanti Empire was formally annexed to the British Crown in 1902
- The confederacy was dissolved
- Chiefs lost most of their power
- Traditional institutions were suppressed
- Over 1,000 Ashanti were exiled

**For the British:**
- The Gold Coast colony was expanded to include Ashanti territory
- A formal colonial administration was established
- The British believed they had finally conquered the Ashanti

### But the Golden Stool Was Never Found

Despite the British victory, they never obtained the Golden Stool. Throughout the war and its aftermath, no one revealed its location. The stool remained hidden, its secret kept by those who had sworn to protect it.

The British eventually claimed that they no longer wanted the stool—an attempt to save face after their disastrous demand had sparked a war. But everyone knew the truth: the Ashanti had defeated the British in this most important respect. The soul of the nation remained safe.

## The Rediscovery of the Golden Stool

### A Shocking Incident

For over twenty years after the war, the Golden Stool remained hidden. Then, in 1920, an extraordinary and troubling incident occurred.

A group of road laborers, working near the village of Bare, accidentally discovered the stool's hiding place. Not fully understanding what they had found, some of them stripped the stool of its gold ornaments.

When word of this sacrilege spread, it caused an enormous crisis. The men who had desecrated the stool were arrested—not by the British, but by Ashanti authorities, who demanded they be tried by traditional law.

### A Delicate Situation

The colonial government faced a dilemma. The incident proved that the Golden Stool still existed and revealed its location. But the British had learned from their mistake in 1900. They realized that any attempt to seize the stool would spark another uprising.

Instead, the government allowed the Ashanti to deal with the matter themselves. The men who had stripped the gold were tried by Ashanti law and executed—one of the few cases where the colonial government permitted the application of traditional capital punishment.

### The Stool Revealed

Following this incident, the Ashanti leaders decided to publicly acknowledge the stool's existence and its continued role in their society. In 1921, the Golden Stool was publicly displayed for the first time since 1896, during a durbar (a formal assembly) in Kumasi.

The British, recognizing the reality of the situation, made no attempt to claim the stool. It remained—and remains—the property of the Ashanti nation.

## The Golden Stool Today

### The Restoration of the Asantehene

In 1924, Prempeh I was finally allowed to return from exile. He came back not as king but as a private citizen—the British having abolished the position of Asantehene.

However, in 1926, the British permitted Prempeh I to be installed as the Kumasihene (chief of Kumasi only). When he died in 1931, the British finally allowed the restoration of the Asantehene. Prempeh II was installed in 1935, with full recognition of the Golden Stool's central role in Ashanti governance.

### The Road to Independence

The story of the Golden Stool became an important symbol in Ghana's independence movement:

- It represented African resistance to colonialism
- It showed that African cultures and institutions had deep value and resilience
- It proved that the colonial powers were not invincible

When Ghana gained independence in 1957—the first sub-Saharan African country to do so—the story of the Golden Stool and the war to protect it was celebrated as part of the national heritage.

### The Golden Stool in Modern Ghana

Today, the Golden Stool remains central to Ashanti identity and governance:

**Status:**
- It is still considered sacred and the embodiment of the Ashanti national soul
- It is the property of the Ashanti people, not of any individual

**Current Asantehene:**
- Osei Tutu II has been Asantehene since 1999
- He was installed in traditional ceremonies involving the Golden Stool
- He maintains the traditional institutions while also being a modern leader

**Public Appearances:**
- The Golden Stool is displayed only at extremely important occasions
- When displayed, it is accompanied by elaborate security and ritual
- Photography is often prohibited
- It is still never sat upon

**Tourism and Education:**
- The Manhyia Palace Museum in Kumasi tells the story of the Ashanti kingdom and the Golden Stool
- Tourists can learn about Ashanti history and culture
- However, the stool itself is not on public display

### Ongoing Significance

The Golden Stool remains relevant in contemporary Ghana:

- It symbolizes Ashanti unity and identity in a modern nation-state
- It represents the importance of cultural heritage
- It serves as a reminder of the resistance to colonialism
- It demonstrates the resilience of African institutions

## Conclusion

### A Story of Resistance and Resilience

The story of the Golden Stool is, at its heart, a story of resistance and resilience. When Governor Hodgson demanded the stool in 1900, he unknowingly touched the very soul of the Ashanti nation. The response—led by the extraordinary Queen Mother Yaa Asantewaa—demonstrated that some things are worth fighting for, regardless of the odds.

The Ashanti people paid a heavy price for their resistance. Their empire was destroyed, their leaders exiled, their traditional institutions suppressed. And yet, in the most important sense, they were victorious. The Golden Stool was never captured. The soul of the nation remained intact.

### Lessons for Today

The story of the Golden Stool offers several lessons for our modern world:

1. **Cultural Heritage Matters**: The Golden Stool was not just a golden object but a symbol of identity and unity. Protecting cultural heritage is worth sacrifice.

2. **Understanding Matters**: The British failure to understand the Golden Stool's significance led directly to an unnecessary war. Cross-cultural understanding is essential for peace.

3. **Resistance Can Succeed**: Even against overwhelming military superiority, resistance can achieve important goals. The Ashanti did not win the war militarily, but they achieved their primary objective—protecting their sacred symbol.

4. **Women Can Lead**: Yaa Asantewaa's leadership proved that women could command in the most challenging circumstances. Her example continues to inspire.

5. **Identity Survives**: Despite colonialism's best efforts, the Ashanti maintained their identity. The Golden Stool survived, and with it, the Ashanti sense of nationhood.

### The Golden Stool Endures

Today, more than three centuries after its legendary descent from the sky, the Golden Stool remains the sacred symbol of the Ashanti people. It has survived wars, colonial conquest, accidental discovery, and the passage of time.

When the current Asantehene appears at major state functions, the Golden Stool is there—not as a museum piece or a historical curiosity, but as a living symbol of a people and their enduring spirit.

The British Empire has long since faded into history. But the Golden Stool endures. And with it, the soul of the Ashanti nation lives on.
